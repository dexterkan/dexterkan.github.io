Meet the deadline.
Share the spare capacity.

A multi-server MEC system jointly decides which tasks to offload, how to allocate radio and computation resources, and when to distribute work across servers. The goal is better user QoS under limited resources.

MY CONTRIBUTION

QoS-aware cost design and resource allocation

What I owned

I developed the research idea and system design, and personally completed the mathematical formulation, algorithm implementation, and simulation experiments.

The core design contribution

I defined a QoS-aware execution cost that accounts for deadline-weighted completion time and penalizes deadline misses or insufficient battery energy. This objective connects scheduling decisions to task requirements rather than average delay alone.

I used Lagrange multipliers to derive computation-resource allocation under the server capacity constraint. The allocation rule is combined with channel assignment and cost-based offloading decisions to form the scheduling mechanism. This derivation solves the resource-allocation subproblem; it does not imply global optimality of the full joint scheduling problem.

01 / THE SYSTEM

Coordinate devices, radio links, and several MEC servers.

Each device has a task, a deadline, remaining battery energy, and local computation capacity. A MEC controller coordinates server resources and redistribution when the serving server cannot accommodate an offloaded task.

Two different offloading boundaries

Device-to-MEC offloading is binary: the whole task either runs on the device or is uploaded. After upload, the controller may place the entire task on one MEC server or divide it into independent instructions across servers.

Partial server distribution finishes when the slowest instruction completes, plus a final gather instruction. The algorithm tries whole-task redistribution first to avoid this extra work.

QoS is more than average delay.

A successful task incurs weighted completion-time cost βT. A missed deadline or exhausted battery incurs a large constant penalty φ. Deadline weights are normalized inverse delay tolerances, giving tighter deadlines greater weight (Equations 3–4).

05 / ASSUMPTIONS & EVALUATION SCOPE

Resource-aware allocation under a simplified system model.

Reported simulation settings

Server computation capacity: [10, 50] × 10⁹; device computation capacity: [1.5, 2.5] × 10⁹; task demand: [100, 2000] × 10⁶ CPU cycles; deadline: [0.1, 1] seconds; local computation power: [5, 10] W (Table I).

Each task may contain 2–4 independent instructions. Figure 4 assumes 1 J to execute 10⁹ CPU cycles and measures nearby-server energy for work originating at MEC Server 1.

Limits of the model

Inter-server transmission time is neglected under a high-rate backhaul assumption. Task outputs are assumed much smaller than inputs, so return-transmission cost is omitted. Split instructions execute independently and simultaneously, followed by a gather instruction.

The results are numerical simulations, not measurements from a deployed MEC system. The energy plot covers redistributed work at nearby servers; backhaul savings are discussed qualitatively rather than directly plotted.
