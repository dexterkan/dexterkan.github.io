MY CONTRIBUTION

Designing the offloading strategy

What I owned

I developed the core research idea and system design, and personally completed the implementation and experimental evaluation. My work covered offloading decisions, scheduling and resource allocation, the simulator, and comparisons against baseline methods.

The central design challenge

The most difficult part was deciding when a task should execute locally, at an edge server, or in the cloud. Faster remote computation must be weighed against transmission, waiting, and coordination delays under task deadlines.

I combined ideas from existing scheduling strategies to develop TODORA’s distributed, threshold-based offloading mechanism. The design couples deadline- and task-size-aware prioritization with resource allocation, making offloading decisions account for the full execution path rather than processor speed alone.

01 / FOLLOW THE TASK

Three execution locations. One shared deadline.

Sending a task to a faster processor can add uplink waiting, transmission, and control-message delay. TODORA evaluates the whole path.

Local check

The vehicle first checks whether it can finish on time while estimated edge upload and computation would miss the deadline. If so, it executes locally without a scheduling request.

Priority combines urgency and task size.

pn = 1 / √(dmaxn Fn). Smaller and more urgent tasks receive higher priority. Compute is shared proportionally to priority; higher-priority waiting tasks get transmission opportunities first.

04 / IMPLEMENTATION & SCOPE

Jointly manage compute, airtime, and mobility.

Scheduling and handoff

The decision compares vehicle, edge, and cloud delay estimates using load information, predicted uplink waiting, and smoothed RTT measurements. The paper’s constant-time complexity claim concerns the per-task threshold decision given available aggregate state.

If a vehicle changes RSU before upload, repeat the local check with the remaining deadline. After upload, let the task finish and forward its result to the vehicle’s new RSU.

Partial offloading

TODORAP extends the binary scheme to split tasks across locations for parallel execution. Reported partial-offloading durations include splitting and result-synthesis overhead.

Evaluation setup

Python 3.8.3 on Ubuntu 20.04.1 LTS; Threadripper 3970X, 32 cores / 64 threads at 3.7 GHz. The base simulation uses 3 RSUs spaced 1 mile apart, 400,000 slots of 10 ms, and Poisson task arrivals with a mean interval of 60 ms.

Vehicle compute: 13–17 TFLOPS; edge: 60–100 TFLOPS; cloud: 600 TFLOPS. Inputs: 6–44 KB; compute demand: 200–1,000 GFLOPs. Vehicle–RSU RTT: 2 ms; RSU–cloud RTT: 10 ms. The modeled LTE uplink is 35 Mbps.

Workloads use Waymo Open Dataset inputs with YOLOv5 detection, Siam R-CNN tracking, MaskFormer panoptic segmentation, and ViTPose++ pose estimation. Compute requirements were profiled on an RTX A6000.

Evaluation boundaries

The offloading results are simulations grounded in task profiles, rather than a deployed vehicular testbed. The model emphasizes GPU FLOPS and assumes known task compute demand. CPU/memory allocation, elastic demand estimation, non-vehicular network load, and deployment-dependent scheduler runtime remain limitations discussed in Section VII.
